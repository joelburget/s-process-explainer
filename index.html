Survival and Flourishing Fund · Funding allocation

What is the S-Process?

A group of evaluators, one pool of money, and honest disagreement about where it should go. The S-Process — short for simulation process — is a deterministic algorithm that turns each evaluator's preferences into a fair final allocation. This page lets you poke at it.

§1 · The problem

Two reviewers, two proposals, one pot

Imagine reviewers A and B, each with $500k to allocate between proposals P and Q. Both reviewers agree completely: P is one person with fixed capacity — the top priority up to $250k, and not a dollar more. Everything after that should go to Q, which can absorb lots of funding.

The same shape shows up whenever several evaluators share a budget — roommates splitting shared expenses, a company allocating compute across projects — with the modeling assumption that funded things have decreasing marginal utility: each additional dollar to the same proposal is worth a bit less.

§2 · Expressing preferences

Tranche tables and the sweep

How do A and B tell the algorithm what they want? With a tranche table. Each row (tranche) says how the next slice of the shared budget should be spent, in priority order. Both reviewers here write the same table:

TranchePQ
1$250k—
2—$750k

Note the totals: even though each reviewer only controls $500k, the table describes what should happen to the whole $1m pool — "if I were in charge of any budget, here's how I'd spend it, best dollars first." This function from budget to allocation is called a unilateral allocation function (UAF).

An equivalent picture is the marginal value function (MVF): each proposal gets a curve of utility-per-dollar against how much it has received. To spend a budget, sweep a horizontal line down from the top — always buying the most valuable dollars available — until the money runs out. Drag the sweep line yourself:

§3 · The two key concepts

Endorsement levels vs. contributions

Run the sweep on both reviewers at once and something pleasant happens: A and B agree exactly on the first $250k (all to P), so they split paying for it — $125k each. They agree again on the remaining $750k and split that too. The final payments are the ideal outcome from §1.

But each reviewer did more than pay $125k toward P. Each one made sure — before letting a single dollar reach Q — that P would receive its full $250k. That is an endorsement level: how much a reviewer ensured a proposal would get, whether or not they were the only one paying for it. The amount actually deducted from a reviewer's budget is their contribution.

A contribution is always ≤ the endorsement level — with n reviewers, an endorsed dollar can cost as little as 1/n of a dollar, when everyone agrees on it.

Proportional contributions

When several reviewers endorse the same dollar, who pays how much of it? The rule is: in proportion to their budgets. Equal budgets, equal split. If A's budget were twice B's, A would cover ⅔ of every shared dollar. Try it:

Why proportional rather than an even split per dollar? With an even split, a $100k reviewer sharing a pool with a $900k reviewer would leave no trace — the outcome would look exactly like the big reviewer's preferences. Proportional sharing keeps the smaller budget's voice audible at its proper volume.

Two reasons to think in endorsement levels rather than contributions:

1. Endorsement levels are what actually happens inside the algorithm — the simulated reviewer walking down your tranche list is setting endorsement levels.
2. Endorsement levels are the part you control. Your tranche table says, for any budget, what you'd endorse. Your contributions depend on everyone else's tables too.

§4 · The algorithm, live

Simulation until fixed point

Contributions are easy to compute from endorsement levels (split every shared dollar proportionally) — but not the other way around. So the algorithm sneaks up on the answer:

Let each reviewer endorse a safe amount — one where the resulting charge can't exceed their budget. Initially that's just the budget itself. Then look at what they were actually charged. If a reviewer endorsed $1,000 but was only charged $750, it's safe to let them endorse $1,250 next round: even in the worst case, the extra $250 falls entirely on them. Each round, every simulated reviewer re-reads their tranche table with this slightly larger virtual budget and adjusts their endorsement levels; charges are recomputed; repeat. The adjustments shrink each round, converging to a fixed point where every budget is exactly spent. Hence simulation process.

A surprising uniqueness result: any procedure that (1) funds the highest-marginal-utility dollars first and (2) shares each jointly funded dollar in proportion to budgets must produce the same output as the S-Process. The two rules pin the answer down completely — the algorithm is just one way of finding it.

§5 · A tempting alternative

Why not just average everyone's tables?

First, averaging isn't even defined until you say how — and the natural definition (convert every table to an MVF, average the curves at each point, convert back) has a real cost. Consider polarized reviewers: A loves P, B loves Q, and both like R a little. Averaged, R's mild broad support beats each favorite's strong narrow support:

Averaged tranchePQR
1——500
210001000—

R receives all $500 before P or Q sees a dollar. Averaging favors proposals that many evaluators like a little, at the expense of proposals that fewer evaluators like a lot.

Both kinds of proposals have merit. So in practice SFF does both: a mean recommender — a synthetic evaluator holding the average of everyone's evaluations — receives some budget, and money also flows through every individual recommender via the S-Process. Broad lukewarm support and strong polarized support can each win some funding.

§6 · FAQ

Why an algorithm at all?

Why not just talk it out?

Often you should! But discussion has failure modes the S-Process avoids. A powerful evaluator can railroad the room. Consensus may simply never arrive, and the S-Process resolves residual disagreement in a principled way. And it's deterministic: record the tranche tables and you can re-run the allocation forever, with a full audit trail of why each proposal got what it did — something a conversation rarely leaves behind.

Do evaluators have to be human?

No — and this is where it gets fun. One recommender wrote a program that grouped project types, ran an S-Process within each group (this nests to any depth), and fed the resulting tranche table in as their evaluation. In the 2025 round, SFF built synthetic matching recommenders — copies of real recommenders' tables adjusted for the extra value of dollars that unlock matching pledges — and gave them budget alongside the humans.

What should I, an evaluator, actually think about?

Your tranche table — that is, your endorsement levels. It's the complete, honest answer to "for any budget, how would you spend it?" The algorithm handles the rest.
